OECM
Okay, let's break down the process for recognizing an area as an "Other Effective area-based Conservation Measure" (OECM).

It's important to understand that the definitive framework comes from the Convention on Biological Diversity (CBD), specifically Decision 14/8 adopted at the Conference of the Parties (COP) 14. National governments are then generally responsible for implementing this framework within their own countries.

Here's a general overview of the process:

1. **Identification of Potential Sites:**

   - This is the first step. Potential OECMs can be identified by various actors, including:
     - National or sub-national government agencies

     - Indigenous Peoples and Local Communities (IPLCs)

     - Private landowners or companies  

     - Non-governmental organizations (NGOs)
   - These are areas that are _not_ currently recognized as formal Protected Areas but appear to be delivering positive and sustained biodiversity conservation outcomes.  

2. **Screening and Assessment against CBD Criteria:**

   - This is the core technical step. The identified site must be evaluated against the official CBD criteria for OECMs. Key [[questions]] include:
     - **Is the area geographically defined?** It must have clear boundaries.

     - **Is it _not_ recognized as a protected area?** OECMs are distinct from formal protected areas (like national parks, nature reserves).  

     - **Is the area governed and managed?** There must be clear governance structures and ongoing management activities.

     - **Does it achieve sustained and effective _in-situ_ conservation of biodiversity?** This is the most critical criterion. It requires demonstrating that:
       - The area contains significant biodiversity values (e.g., important species, habitats, ecosystems, ecological functions).
       - The management practices, _regardless of their primary objectives_ (which could be resource extraction, cultural practices, military use, etc.), are leading to positive conservation outcomes for this biodiversity.
       - These positive outcomes are expected to be sustained over the long term. Evidence and monitoring data are often needed to support this claim.

3. **Verification and Consultation (Often a National Step):**

   - Many countries establish a national process to verify the assessment. This might involve:
     - Peer review of the assessment documentation.

     - Site visits.

     - Consultation with relevant stakeholders, especially IPLCs if they are involved in the governance or management, or if the area affects their rights or traditional territories.  

     - Ensuring Free, Prior and Informed Consent (FPIC) where applicable, particularly concerning IPLCs.  

4. **Documentation:**

   - Detailed information about the site, its biodiversity values, governance, management practices, and evidence of conservation outcomes needs to be compiled.

5. **National Recognition (if applicable):**

   - Some countries may have a formal national register or recognition process for OECMs before reporting them internationally.  

6. **Reporting to the World Database on OECMs (WD-OECM):**

   - Once a site is confirmed to meet the OECM criteria according to the national process (based on CBD guidance), the national government (or an organization delegated by the government) is responsible for reporting it.
   - The data is submitted to the WD-OECM, which is managed by the UN Environment Programme World Conservation Monitoring Centre (UNEP-WCMC) on behalf of the CBD. This is the official global repository.

**Key Considerations:**

- **National Adaptation:** While the CBD provides the international definition and criteria, the specific _process_ (steps, verification methods, responsible bodies) is often defined at the national level. You would need to check the specific procedures within a given country.

- **Evidence is Crucial:** Simply managing an area isn't enough. There must be credible evidence demonstrating _positive and sustained conservation outcomes_.

- **Diverse Governance:** OECMs can be governed by governments, private entities, IPLCs, or combinations thereof. The process should respect the relevant governance structure.  

- **Long-Term Perspective:** The assessment needs to consider whether the positive conservation outcomes are likely to persist in the future.

In summary, the process involves identifying potential sites, rigorously assessing them against internationally agreed criteria (focused on demonstrating effective and sustained biodiversity conservation), often involving national verification and consultation, and finally reporting confirmed sites to the global database (WD-OECM) via the national government.
