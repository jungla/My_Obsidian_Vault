Okay, I think the main point here is that there are multiple ways to be a good leader. There are plenty of examples of multiple different approaches, from the incredibly inclusive people to the sort of solitary dictators, in a way.

I think there are three main aspects to leadership:

- courage
- vision
- charisma ([[On moving people]])

The way this works is that you need a vision, which means you need to know where you want to go, but you need to be able to develop good ideas on how to get there. You need charisma to be able to move people around you and share this vision with others. Then you need courage because often you need to be pushing for the implementation of this vision with the people you have around, and this takes bravery because it's often about uncharted territories.

## Vision

Personally, I like the approach of the knowledgeable leaders, people who are leaders, because they can advise. They know the ins and outs of the problem, and they have a very informed perspective on the issues. This means that it's someone who normally comes from the perspective of actually fixing problems in the first place, hands-on. It's technical people, people that, again, know the details of the issues.

This is also something I tell my teams all the time: I want people that are experts in the technical aspects of the problem. You cannot just be dedicated to one small detail of it, say, for example, be an expert only in, I don't know, procurement procedures. You also need to be familiar with what you're procuring, what that is for, how it fits the bigger context, how your work affects the work of others, and so on.

This is built on true experience. It's the same reason why you cannot be an effective leader from afar, disconnected from the teams. Ideally, you would want to sleep on the floor of the factory, similar to what Elon Musk does. Yes, and people like Elon Musk and Steve Jobs; I think Bezos as well. These are people who are somewhat technical and dive into the technical aspects of issues, and that's, I think, how leadership should be, how leaders should be.

## Courage

Then there's the issue of what being a good leader means. Personally, I think the first aspect of this is being brave and being good at making decisions under stress and in difficult circumstances. It's often about making decisions, having good judgment, and making decisions on issues that are potentially new and that require, again, this depth of understanding of the problems. They can then inform decisions on aspects that are new and that require experience. It requires that knowledge, and then it requires the bravery to actually implement those decisions. You need to trust that that's the right decision, and you need to follow through on that decision.

That's why I think sometimes leadership is sort of an individual responsibility because you need someone on the team who is capable of pushing these things through, to believe that that's the right way. Decisions can be made sort of by one individual, one leader, and that's typical in the case of decisions that need to be made quickly. If you have important decisions, you might want to spend the time to consult the team, and that's what I normally do.

If it's something that is a new problem, one of those pivotal times in, say, the program, where we are deciding now to, for example, start working on monitoring, control, surveillance: we have not done it before, and we don't know where to start from. In a case like that, you probably want to convene a broader meeting with all the people that can potentially contribute to the discussion. Listen to all the perspectives. Be open about what the decision should be, but then you still need to have someone who says, "Okay, now, given all of this information, what is the final outcome of this? How do you decide, and how do you move forward?"
